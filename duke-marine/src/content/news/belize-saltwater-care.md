A trip does not end when the boat comes off the water. Salt, heat and sunlight
keep working on an engine, a reel and a trailer long after the last cast, and
most of the equipment that fails on a Belize boat does not fail out on the
reef. It fails quietly, between trips.

In the second instalment of our On the Water Guide we worked through
[what to check before you leave the dock](/blog/belize-boating-fishing-checklist).
This instalment covers the hour that matters most afterwards.

## Rinse before the salt dries

Salt does the least damage while it is still wet. The sooner you get fresh
water over everything, the less it has a chance to do.

Flush the engine according to its manual, then rinse the hull, deck, transom
and every fitting you touched during the day. Pay attention to the places salt
collects out of sight: under the cowling, around the trim, behind cleats and
chocks, and in the corners of the deck where water sits.

Open the hatches and let air move through. A boat that is rinsed but closed up
wet in Belize heat will grow its own problems.

## The trailer takes the worst of it

A trailer is the only part of the rig that gets dunked in salt and then left in
the sun, and it is the part most often skipped.

Rinse it thoroughly, including the frame, springs, winch cable and the lights.
Check the rollers and bunks for wear, look over the coupling and safety chains,
and keep an eye on the bearings, which give very little warning before they go.
If something on the trailer is beginning to seize, rust through or sit loose, it
is far cheaper to deal with now than on the roadside.

## Dry and store tackle and dive gear properly

Rinse rods, reels, lures and terminal tackle in fresh water and let them dry
fully before anything goes back in a box. Back the drag off before storing a
reel. Run your fingers along the last few metres of line and replace any leader
that has gone cloudy, curled or rough.

Dive gear needs the same treatment. Rinse masks, snorkels, fins and booties,
dry them out of direct sun, and store them where they are not folded or under
pressure. Speargun bands and tubing perish faster than people expect in this
climate; check them for cracking and softness rather than waiting for one to
let go.

## Look at the quiet places

Most of what strands a boat is small and unglamorous. Check your battery
terminals and wiring connections for the green dust that signals corrosion.
Look over switch panels, lights, hose clamps, and the bilge pump and its
plumbing.

Inspect anchor chain, shackles and swivels, and run your hands over dock lines
and fenders. Rope that has gone stiff and chalky is telling you something.
Stainless fittings that have started to weep rust at the base are worth
replacing before they are load-bearing in bad weather.

## Bigger jobs are easier before the season turns

October and November are a sensible time to deal with anything you have been
putting off. Hurricane season runs to the end of November, the first northers
bring rougher water after that, and the busier months follow close behind. Work
done now is done calmly rather than in a hurry.

That is the moment for the jobs that need the boat still: gelcoat and
fibreglass repairs, paintwork, replacing tired rope, refreshing worn trailer
parts and sorting out electrical work you have been living with.
