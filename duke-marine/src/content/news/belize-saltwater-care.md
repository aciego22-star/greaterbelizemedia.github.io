A trip does not end when the boat comes off the water. Salt, heat and sunlight
keep working on an engine, a reel and a trailer long after the last cast, and
most of the equipment that fails on a Belize boat does not fail out on the
reef. It fails quietly, between trips.

Earlier in our On the Water Guide we looked at
[why Belize gives you your best fishing](/blog/why-belize-best-fishing) and at
[what to check before you leave the dock](/blog/belize-boating-fishing-checklist).
This instalment covers the hour that matters most afterwards.

## Rinse before the salt dries

Salt does the least damage while it is still wet. The sooner fresh water goes
over everything, the less chance it has to do its work.

Flush the engine as its manual sets out, then rinse the hull, deck, transom and
every fitting you handled during the day. Pay attention to the places salt
collects out of sight: under the cowling, around the trim, behind cleats and
chocks, and in the corners of the deck where water sits.

Open the hatches and let air move through. A boat that is rinsed but shut up
wet in Belize heat will grow its own problems.

## The trailer takes the worst of it

The trailer is the only part of the rig that gets dunked in salt and then left
in the sun, and it is the part most often skipped.

Rinse it thoroughly, including the frame, springs, winch cable and lights.
Check the rollers and bunks for wear, look over the coupling and safety chains,
and keep an eye on the bearings, which give very little warning before they go.
If something is beginning to seize, rust through or sit loose, it is far
cheaper to deal with now than on the roadside. We carry
[trailer parts](/marine-supplies#trailer) for exactly these jobs.

## Dry and store tackle and dive gear properly

Rinse [rods and reels](/fishing-supplies#rods), lures and terminal tackle in
fresh water and let everything dry fully before it goes back in a box. Back the
drag off before storing a reel. Run your fingers along the last few metres of
[line and leader](/fishing-supplies#line) and replace anything that has gone
cloudy, curled or rough.

Dive gear needs the same treatment. Rinse [masks and snorkels](/diving-supplies#masks),
[fins and booties](/diving-supplies#fins), dry them out of direct sun, and store
them where they are not folded or under pressure. Speargun bands and tubing
perish faster than people expect in this climate, so check them for cracking and
softness rather than waiting for one to let go.
[Replacement bands, barbs and tubing](/diving-supplies#parts) are worth keeping
on hand.

## Look at the quiet places

Most of what strands a boat is small and unglamorous. Check battery terminals
and [wiring connections](/marine-supplies#electrical) for the green dust that
signals corrosion. Look over switch panels, lights, hose clamps, and the
[bilge pump and its plumbing](/marine-supplies#bilge).

Inspect [anchor chain, shackles and swivels](/marine-supplies#anchors), and run
your hands over [dock lines and fenders](/marine-supplies#dock). Rope that has
gone stiff and chalky is telling you something.
[Stainless fittings](/marine-supplies#hardware) that have started to weep rust
at the base are worth replacing before they are load bearing in bad weather.

## Bigger jobs are easier before the season turns

October and November are a sensible time to deal with anything you have been
putting off. The Atlantic hurricane season runs to the end of November, and
Belize’s cold fronts, the northers, become most frequent from December through
February, when they can chop up the water for a day or three at a time. Work
done now is done calmly rather than in a hurry.

That is the moment for jobs that need the boat still:
[gelcoat and fibreglass repairs](/marine-supplies#fiberglass), paintwork,
replacing tired rope and sorting out electrical work you have been living with.
If you are replacing something properly rather than patching it, it is worth
looking at [the brands we stock](/brands) and buying once.
