What Not to Flush or Pour Down Your Drains

The items that quietly damage your septic system, why they cause backups, and the simple habits that protect it.

Maintenance 6 min read · Published August 26, 2026

In our last guide we looked at how often you should pump your septic tank. One of the biggest things that decides that schedule is simple: what you put into the system in the first place. Treat your drains well and you get fewer problems, cleaner pipes, and more time between pump-outs. Here is what to keep out.

The golden rule

A septic system is designed to handle three things: human waste, toilet paper, and water. That is it. Everything else is a guest that overstays its welcome. Solids that do not break down settle in the tank and fill it faster, and some products actually harm the system itself. When you are unsure whether something belongs down the drain, the safest answer is almost always no.

Never flush these down the toilet

The toilet is not a second trash can. These are the most common causes of clogs and backups we see on service calls:

  • Wet wipes, including "flushable" ones. This is the big one. Despite the label, they do not break down like toilet paper. They tangle, catch, and cause blockages. If you use wipes, put them in the bin.
  • Feminine hygiene products. Pads and tampons are made to absorb and expand, which is the last thing you want inside a pipe or tank.
  • Cotton balls, swabs, and dental floss. They do not dissolve. Floss in particular knots together and traps other debris.
  • Paper towels and tissues. They are built to stay strong when wet, so they clog where toilet paper would have dissolved.
  • Diapers and baby wipes. Far too big and absorbent for any drain line.
  • Medications. Flushing old pills does not just risk the system, it puts chemicals into the ground. Dispose of them properly instead.

Never pour these down the sink

The kitchen sink causes just as much trouble as the toilet, usually more slowly and quietly:

  • Cooking grease, oil, and fat. They pour in as a liquid and harden inside your pipes and tank, building up a layer that chokes the flow over time. Let grease cool and scrape it into the trash instead.
  • Coffee grounds and food scraps. They do not break down well and pile up as sludge. A sink strainer and scraping plates into the bin solves this.
  • Starchy and stringy foods. Rice, pasta, and vegetable peels swell or tangle and are common clog culprits.
  • Harsh chemicals. Drain cleaners, large amounts of bleach, paint, and solvents do real damage. More on why below.

Why chemicals are the hidden danger

Here is the part many people do not realise. A septic tank is alive. It relies on natural bacteria to break down the waste inside it. That living process is what keeps the system working between pump-outs. When you pour harsh chemicals down the drain, you kill off those bacteria. With fewer bacteria doing the work, solids stop breaking down, sludge builds up faster, and you end up needing service sooner, or facing a backup. A little household cleaner rinsing through is fine. Dumping bleach, drain cleaner, or solvents is what causes harm.

Simple habits that protect your system

None of this requires effort, just a few small routines:

  • Keep a strainer in every sink and shower drain to catch food and hair.
  • Keep an old can or jar by the stove to collect grease, and bin it once it is full.
  • Scrape plates into the trash before washing.
  • Space out laundry across the week instead of many loads in one day, so you do not flood the system with water at once.
  • Choose septic-safe cleaning and paper products where you can.
  • Keep flushing to the three things the system is built for: waste, toilet paper, and water.

Already seeing a problem?

If your drains are slow, you hear gurgling, or you notice odors, something may already be building up. Do not reach for a chemical drain cleaner, that often makes it worse for a septic system.
